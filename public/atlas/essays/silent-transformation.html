Governance as silent transformation

化 hua against the event · Essay 2 of 5

The English-language coverage of China between roughly 2002 and 2022 produced a peculiar archive. Year after year, prominent magazines and policy journals announced that China was about to undergo a transformative event: a banking crisis, a real-estate collapse, a Tiananmen redux, a regime change, a hard landing, a soft landing, a great opening, a great closing. The events did not arrive. The transformation did. By the time it was unmistakable, no event had marked it; there had only been the slow accumulation of capital stock, infrastructure, manufacturing capacity, urbanization, scientific output, and institutional reach, in something like the way a forest grows back across a generation without any single day on which one could say it had returned.

The pattern is too consistent to be a series of unlucky calls. It is a feature of how event-shaped intelligence reads accumulation-shaped reality. Jullien's Les transformations silencieuses names exactly this kind of change: change that becomes visible only at its end, that cannot be located at any moment, and for which Western thought has no developed vocabulary because Western thought has been, since the Greek crisis-moment and the Christian revelation, organized by the event.

What does it mean to govern by silent transformation?

The Five-Year Plan as a non-event

The Five-Year Plan is, in its outward genre, a planning document. It announces objectives, allocates priorities, and reports against itself five years later. Read as a plan in the Western sense, it is a mediocre plan: many of its targets are missed, others are revised, others are quietly redefined. By Western planning-evaluation standards, the genre is unimpressive.

Read as an instrument of silent transformation, the same document does different work. Its function is less to commit the state to specific outputs than to inflect the propensity of an enormous distributed system — local governments, state-owned enterprises, banks, universities, private firms — by shifting the gradient along which they each pursue their own interests. A line in the plan about, say, indigenous semiconductor capacity is not a deliverable; it is a slope-change. It tells thousands of actors that capital, talent, prestige, and political credit will flow more easily in this direction than the previous one. The actual outcome is the cumulative result of those individual reorientations over many years, most of them invisible at the time.

Western analysis tends to evaluate the plan against its stated targets and call the gap between target and outcome a failure of plan execution. This evaluation is correctly addressing the wrong genre. The plan was not the project; the plan was the incipient moment — the inflection at which a propensity was named and given political cover. The transformation that follows, and that takes a decade to be legible, is the actual policy.

The misreading of "stability"

"Stability" (稳定 wěndìng) is a near-constant in Chinese political vocabulary, and it is regularly read by Western analysts as code for stagnation, repression, or risk-aversion. This too is event-frame thinking. Stability in the silent-transformation register does not mean nothing happens. It means the conditions are maintained under which slow accumulation can continue without rupture. A polity organized for hua does not need or want events; it needs the absence of events that would interrupt the accumulation.

The same word, in the Western frame, is at best a virtue of process and at worst a complacency. The cultural premium on disruption — in tech, in politics, in art — runs the opposite way: events are valuable because they break a complacency that "stability" represents. The two frames are not arguing the same point with different conclusions; they are organized by different ideas of where consequential action lives.

Why "decoupling" became "de-risking"

The U.S.–European debate about economic relations with China shifted, around 2022–2023, from "decoupling" to "de-risking." The shift was almost entirely linguistic, and almost entirely consequential. "Decoupling" presupposes an event: a moment at which the two economies separate, with consequences attributable to that moment and to the officials who decided it. "De-risking" presupposes a transformation: a slow, distributed, ongoing reweighting of supply chains, investment patterns, research collaborations, and standards bodies, the outcome of which will be visible only in retrospect. The first frame demanded a decision and produced political cost without strategic effect. The second frame asks for accumulation and may, over time, produce strategic effect without political cost.

It is worth noticing that the de-risking frame is closer to a hua-organized practice than the decoupling frame ever was. The Western policy system did not arrive at it through any encounter with Chinese strategic thought; it arrived at it through the practical recognition that the event-shaped version was unworkable. Jullien's point is more interesting than the convergence: the resources of hua-thinking were available all along, in a tradition Western policy is institutionally unwilling to consult.

What an event-trained press cannot report

The professional norms of the American press are tuned for events. The lead asks who, what, when, where; the inverted pyramid presupposes a moment around which information can be ranked by salience. These conventions do not produce coverage of accumulation. There is no inverted pyramid for "thirty thousand small inflections over fifteen years."

The result is a systematic asymmetry in coverage. Episodes that fit the event-frame — Hong Kong's 2019 protests, the Galwan clash, the spy balloon, a tariff round, an Apple supply-chain shift — saturate the news cycle. Accumulations that do not — the patient build-out of port concessions, the steady multiplication of joint research centers in Africa, the slow consolidation of regional standards bodies, the year-on-year increase in per-capita rail and metro coverage — appear sporadically, in long-form, with the implicit apology of a story that lacks news. By the time accumulations of this second kind have reorganized a region, no event has covered them, and the public has the right to feel ambushed.

This is not a Chinese trick. The Chinese state would prefer that its silent transformations be silent, but it does not run the American press. The blind spot is endogenous to event-trained intelligence.

The cost of refusing to read accumulation

One could read this essay as an argument for adopting Chinese strategic culture wholesale. That is not the argument. Plenty of what gets done by hua, when the actor is a one-party state with a centralized media environment and weak accountability, is bad: surveillance build-out, ethnic re-engineering, and ecological foreclosure are also silent transformations. The point is narrower. An intelligence community, a media culture, and a political class that recognize only event-shaped change have a structural under-reading of the world, regardless of who their adversary is. The under-reading is not a moral failing. It is a feature of the cognitive equipment, and it predates any rivalry.

The remedy is not to abandon the event. Events are real; they need to be reported and acted on. The remedy is to add — institutionally, cognitively, journalistically — a complementary register tuned for accumulation. That register has names in the Chinese tradition that Western institutions can borrow without converting. Hua is one. Ji, the incipient moment, is another. Their utility does not depend on the politics of their place of origin. They are tools; one can pick them up.

Whether one picks them up or not, the silent transformations continue. The question is only whether one notices them as they happen, or after.
