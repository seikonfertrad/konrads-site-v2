The grammars of time

時 shi-seasonality against chronos and kairos · Essay 4 of 5

"China plays the long game" is the most overused observation in U.S.–China commentary, and it is right in roughly the way a road sign is right: it points in a direction without telling you anything about the terrain. What is the long game made of? On the standard reading, it is the same game everyone else plays — short-termism on the American side, long-termism on the Chinese side, with the difference indexed by the cycles of accountability each polity runs on. Quarterly capitalism and electoral terms produce shorter horizons; one-party rule and lifetime cadre careers produce longer ones.

This is not wrong. But it locates the difference in institutional incentives rather than in any underlying thinking, and so it implies that with sufficient institutional reform an American polity could lengthen its horizon and converge on the Chinese practice. Jullien's Du Temps suggests something subtler: the difference is not only between long and short, but between two grammars of time that organize what counts as duration in the first place.

What the Greeks gave us, and what they didn't

Western thinking about time inherits, principally, two Greek words. Chronos is continuous, homogeneous, abstract — the time of clocks and physics, a backdrop against which events occur and durations measure. Kairos is the decisive moment, the right time to strike, to speak, to convert, to commit. The two are paired and presuppose each other: kairos is the qualitatively distinguished moment within a chronos that is itself qualitatively flat.

This pair has been spectacularly productive. Modern science requires chronos, modern theology and rhetoric require kairos, modern politics oscillates between them — long stretches of administered chronos punctuated by kairos-events of crisis, election, and decision. Even the apparent alternative philosophies of time within the Western tradition — Bergsonian durée, Heideggerian temporality, Deleuzean event — argue against the chronos/kairos pair without escaping its gravitational field.

Classical Chinese thought, as Jullien excavates it, has neither word. It does not lack temporal vocabulary; it has an extensive one. But its base term, shí (時), does not abstract a continuous backdrop from the qualitative content of moments. Shí means season, time-for, right-time, the moment-fit-for-this — and there is no abstract container behind the seasons that they would fill. The seasons are themselves time. There is only the time of planting, the time of harvest, the time of mourning, the time of rest, and these times are constituted by what they are time for, not located against an indifferent line.

The closest Western neighbor of shí is therefore not chronos at all (which has no analog) but kairos in its qualitatively-thick mode — the right moment, the moment whose rightness is internal to it. The cousins are real cousins; Jullien is careful not to overstate the gap. But the asymmetry is also real: kairos in the Greek economy is a marked moment within an unmarked continuum, whereas shí is the texture of time as such. There is no unmarked continuum waiting to be punctuated.

What this does to "the long game"

Once one has the distinction, the cliché about the Chinese long game splits into two different propositions, only one of which is interesting.

The boring proposition is that Chinese institutions, because of cadre tenure and one-party continuity, can plan over horizons American institutions cannot. This is true and trivial. It would also be true of any authoritarian regime with stable succession and would not require any reference to Chinese thought.

The interesting proposition is that the Chinese strategic imagination, even where it does not enjoy long horizons institutionally, organizes time differently. It is more comfortable thinking of the present as a season — as a moment whose character is not "year three of cycle four" but "the time-for these particular openings and closures" — and acting accordingly. Even short Chinese horizons, on this proposition, would feel different from short American ones, because the short horizon would be experienced as a particular season's window rather than as a slice of administered chronos.

Several pieces of empirical texture line up with the second proposition. Chinese strategic writing makes constant reference to "favorable" or "unfavorable" times, "ripening" situations, "moments not yet arrived." The vocabulary maps poorly onto Western strategic prose, where the analog terms have been thinned by economic and managerial discourse into euphemisms for "we're on schedule." The thicker vocabulary survives in some Western practices — agriculture, sailing, certain kinds of medicine — but has been largely expelled from politics and management. The official documents of the U.S. national security apparatus are full of dates, deadlines, milestones, and quarterly reviews. They are almost entirely empty of seasonal vocabulary.

Two consequences for U.S.–China

First: the temptation to fix the U.S. problem by lengthening horizons (more multi-year authorizations, longer planning documents, more permanent agencies) addresses chronos but not shí. Longer chronos is still chronos; it does not produce a strategic imagination at home in seasonal time. A bipartisan fifteen-year industrial strategy that imagines itself as a long version of the four-year strategy will continue to read every quarter as either on or off track, and will continue to be re-litigated whenever the political weather changes. To re-acquire seasonal time as a category of political imagination is a different and much harder task.

Second: the underrated risk in U.S.–China relations may not be the long-game asymmetry but the seasonal-illegibility asymmetry. American officials trained in chronos-kairos repeatedly mistake the Chinese counterpart's seasonal reasoning for either delay (waiting out a deadline) or escalation (the kairos has arrived). Both readings can be wrong simultaneously: the situation may be ripening or de-ripening on a timescale and along axes the chronos-trained reader is not equipped to register. This is not a crisis-stability problem in the standard nuclear-strategy sense; it is closer to a category-error problem in which two strategic cultures are not running on the same temporal grammar at all.

Five-year plans and the dynastic horizon

One last temptation should be resisted: the romantic reading on which Chinese governance plans in dynasties. It does not. The five-year plan is a five-year plan; the leadership turnover is real; the political horizon for any individual leader is not infinite. The seasonal grammar is not a longer kind of clock-time. It is a different organization of strategic attention within whatever horizon one happens to have.

The civilizational self-narration that runs in Chinese official discourse — "five thousand years of continuous civilization" — is a rhetorical resource, not a planning horizon. What it does, when used effectively, is supply a set of seasonal pre-commitments: this is a moment in a long sequence, and it has a character (rejuvenation, recovery, opening, consolidation) that is given by where it sits in the sequence. American political rhetoric has no functional equivalent. "American exceptionalism" supplies a self-image but not a seasonal location; the rhetoric of "this generation" can supply a temporal frame but rarely outlives the speech.

Whether seasonal-time grammar is a strategic asset for China is an open question. It can be: it underwrites patience and produces strategic imagination of a kind chronos-kairos cannot. It can also fail: a government convinced it is in a season of inevitable rejuvenation may be very slow to register that the seasonal reading was wrong. The grammar does not by itself determine outcomes; it shapes the kinds of mistakes one is likely to make.

The corresponding question for the United States is whether a polity organized natively by chronos-kairos can borrow the seasonal grammar without converting to it — can hold, in the same head and the same institutions, the discipline of the deadline and the patience of the season. Jullien's method suggests that this is the right question. The answer will depend less on policy choices than on what intellectual habits the political class is willing to cultivate.
