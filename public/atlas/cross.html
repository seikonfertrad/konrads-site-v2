Where the two maps meet

Cross-map

The two atlases on this site index different fields — comparative philosophy of Asian and Western thought on one side, the LessWrong / Effective Altruism cluster on the other — but they share intellectual gravity. Each is a particular configuration of moral philosophy, decision theory, and futures thinking; each makes its own kind of universalist claim; each has a distinctive blind spot the other can name. This page lays out the cross-map edges: concepts in the Lightcone Atlas paired with their counterparts across the East–West écart.

Method — the conceptual analysis behind the mapping

The cross-map is not a translation table. It is built by a fixed procedure, and knowing the procedure is the only way to read the edges responsibly.

1. Two ontologies, one schema. Each atlas is a small ontology: traditions (historical configurations), thinkers, concepts, disputes, and typed relations between them. The schema is deliberately identical on both sides so that mapping is possible at all — but the two ontologies are not the same kind of object. The East–West Atlas indexes civilizational repertoires stabilized over millennia and read through a century of comparative scholarship; the Lightcone Atlas indexes a living research cluster barely two decades old. Any edge between them therefore connects entities of different maturity, and the edge types are chosen to keep that asymmetry visible rather than flatten it.

2. The relation vocabulary is the analysis. Five edge types are used within each map; only three are permitted across the gap. Écart (after Jullien) marks two concepts that organize the same problem through incompatible configurations — the pairing claims neither equivalence nor opposition, but that each becomes legible against the other. Parallel marks structural resonance without shared lineage or shared problem-framing: the concepts rhyme, and the rhyme is informative, but neither would recognize the other as an answer to its own question. Kinship marks actual shared descent (utilitarianism and the Kantian moral law are both children of European universalism; the edge records the family, not agreement). The two remaining types — pair (mutually constitutive concepts inside one tradition) and lineage (derivation inside one tradition) — are deliberately barred from crossing the gap: using them across the écart would smuggle in the claim that the two configurations form one tradition, which is exactly what the atlas refuses to presuppose.

3. Counterparts are directional by design. Each concept lists the counterparts most salient from its own vantage, and the lists are intentionally not symmetric: ren's page names the moral law because Kant is the interlocutor against which the ren-tradition has had to define itself, while the moral law's page need not name junzi, because junzi is not what the Kantian tradition argues with. Asymmetric salience is a finding about the two configurations, not an error in the data — so the tool now shows the reverse direction separately ("listed as a counterpart by") instead of forcing the lists to match.

4. The residue is part of the result. A mapping that connected everything would be evidence of a broken method. The concepts listed below have no edge across the gap, and the pattern of what fails to map is itself a conclusion of the analysis. On the LW/EA side the unmapped core is the formal decision-theory stratum (Newcomb's problem, CDT/FDT, acausal trade, Pascal's mugging) and population ethics (the total view, the Repugnant Conclusion): the most mathematically articulated layer of the cluster is precisely the layer for which the Chinese tradition offers no counterpart, because it never posed ethics as an aggregation problem over possible persons. On the East–West side, the unmapped concepts are mostly the internal Western metaphysical hinges (Being, eidos, the subject, the will) — which the cross-map does not need, since the LW/EA cluster inherits them silently — and the aesthetic register (dan, the sublime), which the LW/EA cluster simply does not have. A research community with no aesthetics and an inherited, unexamined metaphysics: that is a description the residue produces mechanically, and it is worth having.

Why the cross-map matters

Three observations are worth making explicit. First, the LW/EA cluster's core methodological move — formalize ethics as expected-value over impartially counted welfare across all moral patients — sits at maximum écart from the Confucian role-ethics tradition recovered by Hall–Ames and the relational ren-tradition reread by Jullien. The disagreement is not about which produces better answers in a given case. It is about whether ethics is the kind of thing that admits formalization without remainder. The Lightcone Atlas presupposes that it is; large parts of the East–West Atlas presuppose that it is not.

Second, longtermism's frame of reasoning forward from a desired distant outcome — and from there backward to present action — shares a deep grammar with the Western teleological reflex that Jullien names as the principal écart with Chinese strategic thought. Longtermism is not Aristotelian (it is consequentialist, not virtue-theoretic), but it is plan-shaped at cosmic scale, where shi-thinking would read the cosmic situation's propensity instead. The strongest Chinese-tradition critique of longtermism is not that it cares about the future but that it imagines the future as a state to be reached rather than as a propensity to be inflected.

Third, AI alignment as currently framed treats values as objects that can be encoded, transferred, and optimized for. This is the formalization-of-ethics premise applied to a new substrate. Wuwei and ren — and aidagara, in the Kyoto School lineage — name an alternative in which appropriate behavior is the residue of cultivated relation, neither encoded nor objective-shaped. Whether the alignment frame can absorb the relational alternative (or has to) is a philosophical question the field rarely surfaces. The cross-map edge from ai-alignment to wuwei is the place that question lives.

These are not arguments that the East–West tradition is right and the LW/EA tradition is wrong. They are the écart, in Jullien's sense — the productive gap from which both configurations become legible. The atlas's bet is that holding both visible at once, without dissolving either, sharpens specific perceptions that running either alone could not.
