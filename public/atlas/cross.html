Where the two maps meet

Cross-map

The two atlases on this site index different fields — comparative philosophy of Asian and Western thought on one side, the LessWrong / Effective Altruism cluster on the other — but they share intellectual gravity. Each is a particular configuration of moral philosophy, decision theory, and futures thinking; each makes its own kind of universalist claim; each has a distinctive blind spot the other can name. This page is the mapping between them: the problems both configure, the edges that pair their answers, the disputes that rhyme across the gap, and the residue that refuses to map.

The axes — problems both configurations answer

Every concept edge across the gap runs along one of these axes. An axis is a question; its two poles are the rival ways of configuring it; the chips are the concepts, from either map, that sit at each pole. Two concepts on opposite poles of an axis are in écart; two on the same pole, reached from different lineages, are parallel. The edge types below are derived from these positions, not asserted, and the audit script refuses an edge that contradicts its axis.

The residue — what does not map, and why

A mapping that connected everything would be evidence of a broken method. The concepts below have no edge across the gap, and each states why. The pattern is the finding: on the LW/EA side, the unmapped core is the formal decision-theory stratum and population ethics — the most mathematically articulated layer of the cluster is exactly the layer for which the Chinese tradition offers nothing, because it never posed ethics as an aggregation problem over possible persons. On the East–West side, the unmapped concepts are mostly the Western metaphysical hinges the LW/EA cluster inherits silently and never names, plus the aesthetic register it does not have.

Method — the conceptual analysis behind the mapping

The cross-map is not a translation table. It is built by a fixed procedure, and knowing the procedure is the only way to read the edges responsibly.

1. Two ontologies, one schema. Each atlas is a small ontology — traditions, thinkers, concepts, disputes, typed relations — and the schema is identical on both sides so that mapping is possible at all. But the two are not the same kind of object: one indexes civilizational repertoires stabilized over millennia and read through a century of comparative scholarship; the other indexes a living research cluster barely two decades old. The edge types are chosen to keep that asymmetry visible rather than flatten it.

2. Problems before concepts. A concept is an answer, and an écart is legible only against a shared question. So the mapping's primary layer is the set of axes above: the problems both configurations answer. Every concept edge cites the axis it runs along, and its type — écart or parallel — is derived from where the two concepts sit. This replaced an earlier version in which types were assigned by intuition; the derivation retyped fourteen edges, all of them "parallels" that on inspection paired rival answers to one question. Kinship is the one type not derived: it asserts shared descent and must name the lineage. Pair and lineage are barred from crossing the gap, because using them there would smuggle in the claim that the two configurations form one tradition.

3. Plurality on both sides. The Billeter objection applies to the mapping itself: it is easy to pit "the Chinese tradition" against LW/EA. The East–West atlas therefore includes the classical tradition's own dissenters where they matter most for this comparison — Mozi, whose impartial care is the closest pre-modern analog of Bentham–Singer impartiality anywhere, and Xunzi, the Confucian who reasons like an alignment researcher — and the dispute edges map their quarrels with Mencius onto the LW/EA cluster's own internal debates. Several edges that an earlier version drew as gaps between "China" and EA are now, more accurately, within-Chinese disputes with an EA parallel.

4. The right West. The East–West atlas's Western pole was configured for Jullien's contrast with Greek and Kantian thought; read against it, the LW/EA cluster looked ancestorless. The Lightcone atlas now carries the lineage the cluster actually descends from — Hume's fact/value gap, Sidgwick's point of view of the universe, Jaynes's probability as logic — and the one documented point where the two Western lineages meet.

5. Both directions, and provenance. Every edge carries a reading from each side: not only what the Chinese concept reveals about the LW/EA one (the détour), but what the LW/EA concept says back. And every edge states its basis. Only five concept edges are textual; the rest are this atlas's analytic claims, and a handful are marked speculative. The honest description of the cross-map is a proposed conversation that has not yet taken place.

6. Counterparts are directional; the residue is a result. Each concept lists the counterparts most salient from its own vantage, and the lists are deliberately not symmetric — the tool shows the reverse direction separately. Concepts with no relation across the gap must say why, and the audit script (tools/atlas-audit.mjs) rejects an unmapped concept without a stated reason and a mapped concept with a stale one.

Why the cross-map matters

Three observations are worth making explicit. First, the LW/EA cluster's core methodological move — formalize ethics as expected-value over impartially counted welfare across all moral patients — sits at maximum écart from the Confucian role-ethics tradition recovered by Hall–Ames and the relational ren-tradition reread by Jullien. The disagreement is not about which produces better answers in a given case. It is about whether ethics is the kind of thing that admits formalization without remainder. The Lightcone Atlas presupposes that it is; large parts of the East–West Atlas presuppose that it is not. And the classical tradition had this argument itself, between the Mohists and the Confucians, before either side of the modern one existed.

Second, longtermism's frame of reasoning forward from a desired distant outcome — and from there backward to present action — shares a deep grammar with the Western teleological reflex that Jullien names as the principal écart with Chinese strategic thought. Longtermism is not Aristotelian (it is consequentialist, not virtue-theoretic), but it is plan-shaped at cosmic scale, where shi-thinking would read the cosmic situation's propensity instead. The strongest Chinese-tradition critique of longtermism is not that it cares about the future but that it imagines the future as a state to be reached rather than as a propensity to be inflected.

Third, AI alignment as currently framed treats values as objects that can be encoded, transferred, and optimized for. This is the formalization-of-ethics premise applied to a new substrate — and it is also Xunzi's premise, that the good must be made. Wuwei and ren, and aidagara in the Kyoto School lineage, name an alternative in which appropriate behavior is the residue of cultivated relation, neither encoded nor objective-shaped. Whether the alignment frame can absorb the relational alternative, or has to, is a philosophical question the field rarely surfaces. The dispute edge from the Mencius–Xunzi quarrel to the alignment-target debate is where that question lives.

These are not arguments that the East–West tradition is right and the LW/EA tradition is wrong. They are the écart, in Jullien's sense — the productive gap from which both configurations become legible. The atlas's bet is that holding both visible at once, without dissolving either, sharpens specific perceptions that running either alone could not.
