How frontier AI coordination works — and why it doesn't

A structured description of the system that governs cooperation on advanced artificial intelligence between the world's two leading AI powers. It has two layers: a causal map of how the forces in the system drive one another, shown first, and below it the six conditions that hold the situation in place. Written for readers coming to the subject fresh — every term is explained where it first appears, and no familiarity with AI policy is assumed.

Reading the map

The six conditions described further down set out what holds the situation in place. On their own they do not show how the parts interact — and most of the difficulty in this system comes from interaction rather than from any single condition. The diagram above breaks the six conditions into 65 individual factors and draws the causal relationships between them.

What a diagram like this is for. It is a causal loop diagram, a standard tool for describing systems that resist change. Its purpose is not to predict, but to make visible the circuits through which a situation sustains itself — so that the difference between a symptom and a cause becomes arguable rather than assumed. Reading it should take a few minutes; hovering over any factor isolates the chains it belongs to.

Reinforcing and balancing loops. Some chains of cause and effect feed back into themselves and accelerate: more of one thing produces more of another, which produces more of the first. These are reinforcing loops, and they are why some problems get worse on their own. Others push back against change and hold a situation at equilibrium; these are balancing loops, and they are why some problems resist solution even when everyone involved wants them solved. This system contains three of each — and, as the map shows, they are not evenly matched.

A note on the statement at the centre. A causal diagram needs a reference point: the factors have to point at something for the arrows to mean anything. The statement below describes what this system would look like if it were working — not a policy proposal or a route to get there, but a stipulated description of a functioning state, used so that each factor can be read as either moving the system toward it or holding the system away from it.

The outcome this system bears on

Frontier AI develops within a system where leading companies and governments have the relationships, incentives, and governance infrastructure needed to cooperate effectively, manage shared risks, and build mutual confidence. Strategic competition is balanced by mechanisms for transparency, coordination, and accountability, enabling powerful AI capabilities to develop responsibly without reinforcing avoidable risks or excessive concentrations of power.

Read as a description of a functioning state rather than a proposal. It names companies and governments as the actors, which is why the capability loop carries as much weight in the map as the state-rivalry one. It asks for relationships, incentives and governance infrastructure — three distinct things, of which the map shows infrastructure as the thinnest. And it treats strategic competition as a given to be balanced rather than removed: a system that competes safely, which is a narrower and more achievable thing than one that cooperates.

What the loop structure shows. Three of the six dynamics are reinforcing and three are balancing, but they are not evenly matched. The reinforcing loops — competition without coordination, capability outrunning oversight, and the way beliefs get formed — run on their own momentum and contain no internal brake. The balancing loops all depend on something being supplied: an architecture that must be built, trust that must be established, exchange that someone must pay the cost of. Nothing supplies them automatically, which is why the balance does not hold by default.

Three places the structure concentrates. The pacing problem is the "bottom" the competitive dynamic runs toward — not a race to be less safe deliberately, but one in which nobody can afford to wait for the safety work to catch up. Accuracy of the governance worldview sits underneath almost everything: not how much is known, but whether the people doing the thinking have it roughly right, which more participants alone does not fix. And independent evaluators is the clearest hole on the oversight side — the function barely exists, and will not appear without liability, insurance or a regulator to pay for it.

Comprehension and trust are separated deliberately. They are routinely run together, and they are not the same problem. Misreading the other side's behaviour is a comprehension failure; not believing a signal you have understood correctly is a trust failure. They respond to different interventions, and one may be considerably more tractable than the other — which is only visible if the map keeps them apart.

Start here

The problem in one paragraph.

The most capable AI systems in the world are built by a small number of companies, almost all of them in the United States and China. These systems are advancing quickly, and some of the risks they create — misuse in biology or cyberattacks, accidents in systems nobody fully understands, sudden concentrations of economic and military power — do not stop at national borders. Managing risks of that kind normally requires countries to cooperate: to share information, agree standards, verify each other's claims, and maintain channels that keep working during a crisis. Very little of that machinery currently exists for AI, and the two countries that would need to build it are strategic rivals. This document describes the conditions that hold that situation in place.

A note on what this is and is not. This is a description, not a proposal. It sets out what the system looks like and which way each part of it appears to be moving. It does not argue for a particular policy, and it does not assess any organisation working in the field.

It is also a qualitative account. The readings here are informed judgements drawn from practitioners working directly in this area, not measurements. They are offered as a structured starting point for discussion, and reasonable people working in the field disagree about several of them.

The vocabulary, briefly

Six terms do most of the work in what follows. The rest are explained as they arise.

  • Frontier AI — the most capable AI systems in existence at any given moment, built by a handful of well-resourced companies often called frontier labs. The frontier moves; what counts as frontier today will be ordinary in a few years.
  • Compute — the specialised computer chips and data centres needed to build and run these systems. Compute is expensive, physically concentrated, and traceable, which makes it unusually visible compared with software.
  • Open-source models — AI systems whose underlying components are published for anyone to download and run. Once released they cannot be recalled, and safety controls applied by the original developer no longer apply.
  • Evaluations, or "evals" — structured tests of what an AI system can do and where it fails, particularly on dangerous capabilities. Assurance is the broader practice of establishing, and being able to demonstrate, that a system is safe enough to deploy.
  • Track 1 and Track 2 — terms from diplomacy. Track 1 is official government-to-government negotiation. Track 2 is unofficial dialogue between academics, researchers and former officials, which can explore ideas that governments cannot yet discuss formally. Track 1.5 sits between the two, mixing serving officials with unofficial participants.
  • Export controls — national laws restricting which technologies may be sold abroad and to whom. Since 2022 the United States has used them extensively to limit China's access to advanced AI chips.

The framework used here

Six conditions, at three depths.

The description below is organised using a framework from the study of systems change, known as the Six Conditions of Systems Change (Kania, Kramer and Senge, 2018). Its argument is that any persistent situation is held in place by six kinds of condition, operating at three levels of visibility — and that the least visible ones are the hardest to change but matter most.

Structural conditions are explicit and can be read off the public record: the policies that formally govern the field, the practices institutions actually follow day to day, and the resource flows of money, people and knowledge. Relational conditions are semi-explicit — the relationships between actors, and the power dynamics that determine whose decisions count. The transformative condition is mental models: the assumptions people hold without examining them, which quietly shape everything above.

The practical value of the framework is that it separates things that are usually run together. A shortage of agreements is a policy problem; not knowing who to negotiate with is a relationship problem; believing the other side cannot be trusted is a mental model. They look similar from a distance and respond to entirely different interventions.

Structural conditions

Explicit

The visible architecture: formal rules, the everyday working practices of institutions, and how money, people and knowledge move.

1

Policies

The formal laws, regulations and rules that govern the field.

What this condition contains

  • There is no binding international agreement on frontier AI safety, and no mechanism to enforce one if it existed. This is the single most consequential feature of the landscape. Comparable technologies — nuclear materials, chemical weapons, aviation — acquired international rules and inspection regimes over decades; AI has neither.
  • Attention is the scarce input. Most government attention in both countries is occupied by other things. Policy tends to move only when concentrated attention lands on one problem and one credible solution at the same moment, and such moments are rare.
  • The deeper obstacle is uncertainty rather than unwillingness. Where the technology is heading is genuinely unclear, and it is not obvious what a good rule would say. Both governments treat AI as economically consequential enough that they would rather move slowly than badly — a different problem from one where the answer is known and blocked.
  • Rules are multiplying without joining up. National regimes are diverging, and competing international bodies are being established rather than converging ones, with pressure on third countries to align with one side or the other.
  • The European Union's AI Act is the leading binding regime and reaches beyond Europe: companies elsewhere adjust their practices to retain access to the European market. Whether its penalties will be enforced in practice, and with what diplomatic consequences, is untested.
  • Export controls restrict by confusion as well as by design. The rules are long and highly technical, most affected organisations never read them closely, and behaviour ends up governed by cautious guesswork. Self-imposed restriction routinely goes further than the law requires.
  • Both governments regulate AI chiefly to encourage its development rather than to manage risks from the most advanced systems. China's rules focus on deployment and content; the United States has no comprehensive federal AI law, and binding requirements come mainly from individual states. On both sides, work that touches national security is largely kept out of public view, so activity that does occur may not be visible from outside.
  • Neither state has settled institutional machinery for this. Governance capacity is thin on both sides — China has fewer specialists in AI safety specifically, while the US government holds little of the relevant technical expertise itself and relies heavily on the companies it would oversee.
  • Technical standards are the most genuinely international layer. Standards bodies are slow, but they are a working venue where actors from different countries negotiate detail, and industry participates actively.

Which way it is moving

Toward more rules and less coherence, but slowly, and for a reason easily misread. The volume of AI policy is rising while the connections between regimes thin out. The brake is not opposition so much as rationed attention and genuine doubt about what to require. Two counter-currents run the other way: the European regime is changing behaviour in companies never subject to it, and the international standards track continues to function.

2

Practices

The shared activities and ways of working of institutions, professionals and coalitions.

What this condition contains

  • Voluntary company practice is becoming law. Commitments that leading developers first adopted by choice have been written into legislation in some jurisdictions — evidence that the informal-to-formal pathway does work, given time.
  • But legal requirements have outrun the ability to meet them. Frameworks increasingly specify oversight that nobody yet knows how to perform. The clearest example concerns AI systems that act autonomously and in groups: reviewing their records does not reliably reveal what they did, because there is no way to confirm the records are complete, and no established method for monitoring several such systems operating together. Much of what current rules ask for cannot presently be delivered.
  • Official dialogue is fragile. The government-to-government channel exists and matters, but the relationship has worsened, and its agenda covers many subjects beyond AI — so AI discussions are shaped by considerations that have nothing to do with AI. It produces bursts of attention that arrive and disappear.
  • Unofficial dialogue is short of participants, not oversupplied. It can appear crowded because several organisations convene overlapping groups, but the underlying constraint is a shortage of people taking part in exchange at all. The formats closest to government are the thinnest.
  • Nobody is confident who the key decision-makers are — and neither government is certain for itself. Responsibilities are new, contested between departments, and frequently reassigned. A great deal of what looks like ineffective dialogue follows from this.
  • Meetings often depend on having a pretext. Participants who want to attend will do the work to get there, but travel frequently requires an official occasion to justify it. Established international meetings function partly as permission to travel. This has been stable for some time.
  • Seniority and substance trade off against each other. High-level formats carry weight but often lack technical experts; junior formats can be convened more easily and allow ideas to be passed upward over time, but carry less authority. Long-running senior dialogues largely date from a period when they were easier to establish.
  • Large international summits shape expectations. A handful of conferences have had an outsized effect on what participants believe about where the field is going, though they tend to register shifts in the political mood rather than create them.
  • Inside the companies, safety practice is self-defined, and internal use of new systems tends to run ahead of any oversight of that use.
  • The companies are not yet behaving like international actors. They are investing more in engagement abroad, but without the standing relationships or crisis-communication channels the role implies, and with coarse working models of how other countries' systems operate.
  • Practices carry the marks of their origin. Almost all originate in the United States or China, which affects how readily they are adopted elsewhere.
  • Independent researchers supply much of the thinking. Proposals for what good practice should look like come disproportionately from research institutes, independent analysts and public writers rather than from governments. Whether states adopt them is a separate question.
  • Testing and assurance are becoming a governance frontier, with independent evaluators and capability trackers maturing. Sharing evaluation results across borders remains rare. Chinese government capacity to assess frontier risks is thin, and US government capacity depends largely on access the companies choose to grant.
  • Verification and trusted-intermediary arrangements are emerging but marginal. Proposals exist for independently verifying claims about AI systems, and for neutral facilities where sensitive material could be examined without either party taking possession. These are live design questions held by a small number of specialists rather than mainstream activity.

Which way it is moving

Practice is being invented faster than it can be carried out. The gap between voluntary norms and law is narrowing; the gap between what rules require and what anyone knows how to do is widening. Meanwhile the formats that carry cross-border exchange depend on pretexts and on individual conveners, so the field tends to rebuild its containers rather than accumulate them.

Resource Flows

How money, people, knowledge and information are allocated across the field.

What this condition contains

  • Computing power is concentrated, and is the most governable point in the system. Building frontier systems requires enormous quantities of specialised chips, held by a small number of organisations. Because compute is physical, expensive and traceable, most serious proposals for verifying what AI developers are doing end up focusing on hardware.
  • Open-source release is an emerging complication that returns to the same point. Freely available models can carry real risk and cannot be withdrawn once published. But the practical control point remains compute: overseeing how systems are actually used means knowing who is running them, which means knowing where the hardware is — which draws in data centres and energy suppliers.
  • Funding for AI safety and governance work comes from a narrow base, weighted heavily toward philanthropy in the United States, with few funders and little comparable money elsewhere.
  • That funding is tied to the industry it examines. Much of the money supporting governance work ultimately derives from the commercial success of AI companies. This shapes which ideas attract resources, independently of their merit.
  • Technical talent is being absorbed into the companies, and with it the expertise required to assess them. The pool of independent technical depth available to governments, researchers and civil society is shrinking.
  • Knowledge is becoming private. Confidentiality agreements and hiring have quieted researchers who previously published openly, so detailed understanding of the most advanced systems increasingly exists only inside the organisations building them.
  • The pool of people able to work across borders is growing slowly, and is far outpaced by demand. It remains small and shared, with the same individuals invited by multiple organisations, and travel between the two countries is difficult in both directions.
  • The underlying economics are uncertain. Supply-chain constraints and an unresolved question about whether current investment levels are sustainable both bear on how the technology develops.

Which way it is moving

Every flow is concentrating, and the concentrations reinforce one another: compute attracts capital, capital attracts talent, talent carries knowledge inside, and knowledge held inside makes external assessment harder — which raises the value of being inside. Nothing in the system currently works against this, and the organisations that could are the ones it benefits.

Relational conditions

Semi-explicit

The quality of connection between actors, and how influence is distributed among them — felt more often than documented.

4

Relationships & Connections

The quality and reach of connection and communication between actors, especially across boundaries.

What this condition contains

  • It is unclear who holds the authority to decide — on both sides, and more fundamentally than it first appears. Many of the relevant roles did not exist a few years ago. In some cases the authority genuinely has not been assigned; in others it exists but is unknown outside, has been moved between departments, or is actively contested. It can change over short periods.
  • Access runs through trusted intermediaries rather than through relevance alone. Getting an idea in front of Chinese policymakers generally requires routing it through an institution the government already trusts; having a pertinent point is not sufficient. Access in the United States also runs through trusted intermediaries — established think tanks, former officials and personal networks — and depends heavily on demonstrating immediate usefulness. In both cases personal trust carries further than formal channels.
  • Distrust is not the only barrier, and may not be the main one. Practical obstacles operate independently: different national priorities, language, and counterparts whose roles simply do not correspond to one another. These would remain even if political relations improved.
  • There is a comprehension gap that a thaw would not close. Each side struggles to read the other accurately — on the Chinese side, difficulty tracking developments in both the American government and its technology industry, which operate on different logics; on the American side, scarce Chinese-language expertise and a tendency to read Chinese policy through a small number of translated documents. Because this gap is not caused by the political temperature, better relations would not resolve it.
  • Companies and governments are becoming entangled, asymmetrically. Both countries are drawing closer to their own AI industries, but in different ways and at different speeds, and alignment is increasingly being organised into blocs around access to hardware and software. Which alignments matter varies by subject, and older alliance structures still carry much of the actual exchange.
  • The path by which expertise reaches government is remarkably narrow. On the most technical questions, officials often consult a very small number of individuals, who in turn draw on informal networks of researchers concentrated in one part of the world. An enormous amount rests on a handful of people.
  • Most thinking about frontier risk originates in one place. That it comes overwhelmingly from a small community in California makes it harder for actors elsewhere to adopt — and raises the open question of how other countries develop approaches they genuinely own rather than import.
  • Decision-making networks are becoming more crowded and more specialised as AI intersects with biology, cybersecurity, nuclear policy and finance, each pairing generating its own expert bodies and committees.
  • Official channels are thin and conditional, and the researcher-to-researcher links that once supplemented them are fraying as publication becomes rarer.
  • Company-to-company contact does not always require governments. Depending on the subject, direct engagement between firms across the divide is possible.

Which way it is moving

Connections between states are thinning while connections between companies and their own governments thicken, and alignments are hardening around the technology itself. The net effect is that the most consequential relationships in the system increasingly have no diplomatic conventions attached to them, are held by organisations with no tradition of maintaining contact through a crisis, and often run through a small number of individuals whose position is neither formal nor stable.

Power Dynamics

The distribution of decision-making authority, legitimacy and informal influence.

What this condition contains

  • Power divides by subject, and conflating the two halves causes confusion. Decisions about how frontier systems are actually built and tested rest with a very small number of company leaders, and governments have little practical influence over them. But the terms on which the technology is governed are still set by states: governments define what companies must comply with, not the reverse. Voluntary disclosure regimes that every major developer treats as effectively obligatory illustrate the point.
  • The companies lack the machinery to use the influence they have. Even where they hold effective power, they have no established apparatus for engaging governments, international institutions and publics — a limitation they are increasingly recognising themselves.
  • The current concentration may not last. How long the frontier remains the dominant source of economic value is genuinely unclear, even though it will remain a source of risk. Widespread automation of physical work, or highly capable freely available models, would move where value accumulates and therefore where power sits. Developments worth watching include unemployment at levels that would destabilise political systems, vulnerabilities in financial and infrastructure systems if software becomes unreliable, and control over the narratives through which populations understand what is happening.
  • There are hard limits on how far either side's decision-making can be understood. Significant decisions on national security run through closed structures in both countries: classified processes in the United States, and in China structures closed to foreign observers. American processes are more visible overall, through legislation, hearings and the press, but its most sensitive decisions are just as hidden, and some on both sides may never be documented publicly. These are lasting boundaries rather than gaps that effort will close.
  • Political systems churn, and officials price that in. Authority shifts with electoral cycles and leadership changes on both sides. In the period before an expected reshuffle, officials become markedly more cautious, since a visible mistake carries career consequences.
  • Companies are governed by commercial incentives on both sides. This is why firms with revenue in Europe attend closely to European rules while firms without it do not.
  • Both governments face public-opinion constraints, and both cultivate a national narrative of technological leadership, but the pressure is expressed differently: through elections, partisan polarisation and lobbying in the United States, and in China through public expectations of continued economic growth and technological progress. Either way it shapes how the trade-off between innovation and caution is struck.
  • The bilateral relationship carries far more than AI. Positions on AI move for reasons rooted in trade, security and domestic politics that have nothing to do with the technology.
  • The two systems are organised differently, so officials who appear equivalent often differ in authority, remit and seniority — which means convening the apparent counterparts does not reliably produce a functioning discussion.
  • Decisions are moving inside institutions. As stakes rise, consequential choices are made within organisational boundaries rather than in open forums where outside actors have standing.
  • Neutral ground is scarce and contingent. The venues and hosts regarded as impartial depend on those states remaining both relevant to AI and credibly non-aligned — a position that political decisions elsewhere could weaken.
  • Much of the world is absent. Countries outside the United States, China and a handful of others are thinly represented in frontier AI governance, despite bearing many of the consequences.

Which way it is moving

Toward a split that neither common account captures. Effective power over what the most advanced systems do has moved into private hands and sits with very few people. Formal power over the terms of governance has not moved, but rests with states that are slower, thinner on expertise, and drawing their technical understanding from the very organisations they would regulate. The whole distribution is contingent on a technological picture that may not hold: if the frontier ceases to be where the value is, this condition reorganises.

Transformative condition

Implicit

The deeply held, often unspoken assumptions that shape everything above them.

6

Mental Models

Habits of thought and taken-for-granted assumptions that hold the whole system in place.

The dominant assumptions

  • "The other side cannot be trusted" — which is a different and harder problem than believing the other side will not cooperate. Even when one party signals willingness, the other tends to doubt the signal or see no way to act on it. There is a track record behind this: both have had interests they would have preferred to resolve and did not manage to. The belief is self-confirming, because each side's resulting caution reads to the other as proof it was right.
  • AI as a zero-sum race in which restraint means falling behind, and security concerns outrank safety ones.
  • An assumption that the old security order is ending. International stability was long organised around nuclear deterrence; AI is widely assumed to be displacing that arrangement, without a settled account of what replaces it. The competition is also no longer understood as primarily military — economic capability is now treated as an instrument of strategic rivalry in its own right.
  • That humans are the most intelligent thing there will be. Held by most senior decision-makers without examination, and consequential: if the assumption does not hold, the risk picture changes substantially, which is precisely why it limits how seriously extreme scenarios are taken.
  • A general tendency to discount extreme risks. Beyond any difference between national cultures, low-probability high-severity events are systematically underweighted by individuals and institutions alike.
  • Different framings of what AI is for. American discussion tends to approach AI through national competition and, in parts of the field, risk and safety; Chinese discussion primarily through economic development, alongside a smaller but active safety research community. Each side tends to underestimate how much the other shares its concerns. This shapes what each side will discuss, and with whom.
  • Competing narratives about the other's motives. One holds that the United States seeks dominance while China offers a more cooperative model; another that democracies must prevail over authoritarian states. Underlying claims about fundamental value differences are widely believed — and the belief itself, whatever its accuracy, shapes behaviour.
  • Safety concerns are received as Western in origin. From outside the countries where the discourse developed, the framing of AI risk can appear as an external agenda rather than a shared concern, which affects how readily it is taken up.
  • AI's significance is assessed very differently around the world. The view that AI is not merely the technology of the decade but a civilisational turning point is held strongly in parts of the United States and hardly at all elsewhere. Much international discussion is conducted between parties who do not share a premise about what is at stake.
  • Safety and competitiveness as opposed rather than compatible — though this is beginning to shift, partly under regulatory pressure.
  • Verification read as enforcement. Technical proposals to verify claims about AI systems are frequently heard in China as export controls by another name, just as Chinese proposals for UN-centred governance are often heard in the United States as an attempt to dilute American influence. Each shapes reception regardless of intent.

Where the disagreement is

  • Where the risk actually sits — in how the most advanced systems are developed, or in how AI meets society at scale through employment, information and the concentration of economic power. The field currently weights the first far more heavily, and that is a choice rather than an established finding.
  • How soon. Expectations about when highly capable systems arrive differ by an order of magnitude between serious participants, with no shared basis for settling the question.
  • Whether to extrapolate at all. Whether one extends current trends forward, or treats such extrapolation as unserious, has proved consequential: those who took rapid progress seriously shaped the field — and, depending on the choices they made, also accelerated it.
  • Unresolved normative questions. Whether AI displacing human work and judgement is desirable, and whether these systems could have morally relevant interests of their own, are now raised openly and without settled positions.
  • Whether coordination is even the objective. Much of the system is organised around managing competition rather than solving shared problems, and many participants treat that as the realistic ceiling rather than a stage on the way to something more.
  • What can be said. The range of discussable positions moves: topics that were briefly unmentionable in policy circles have become sayable again, which changes what can be proposed.
  • The inverted question. Using AI to solve governance problems — strengthening defences against biological threats, for instance, rather than only restricting AI's biological capabilities — is comparatively unexplored, and may matter more if timelines are short.

Which way it is moving

The dominant framing is not weakening, but it is becoming less total. Safety is discussable again in places where it briefly was not, regulation in one jurisdiction is changing behaviour in another, and there are individual subjects on which cooperation is unusually welcome. At the same time the framing is broadening in ways that make it harder to argue against, as the competition is redefined beyond the military and the order it supposedly replaces is assumed to be finished. These are cracks in an assumption rather than a replacement for it: no alternative account currently has comparable reach.

What the pattern suggests

The system is held hardest at its shallowest and its deepest layers.

The six conditions are not equally fixed. Two of them — resource flows and mental models — are rigid for the same underlying reason: each is a self-reinforcing cycle with nothing inside it pushing the other way. Computing power attracts investment, which attracts talent, which carries expertise inside the organisations that already hold the advantage. The belief that rivalry is inevitable justifies the behaviour that confirms the belief. Neither corrects itself, and both accelerate.

Practices is the loosest condition and the only one where genuinely new things are being invented. That looseness is real but shallow: the field is writing rules for work nobody yet knows how to do. Relationships is changing shape rather than simply tightening — connections between states thin while connections between companies and governments thicken, so the system's connective tissue is being rebuilt around actors with no conventions for maintaining it. Power dynamics reads as less fixed than it first appears once it is separated by subject: the terms of governance have not left the state, and private concentration depends on the frontier remaining where the value is.

One caveat on the whole description. Framing this as a relationship between two countries reflects where the most capable systems are currently built, and is useful for that reason. But it is a choice of lens, and it has a cost: describing a situation as fundamentally bilateral can make it more adversarial, reinforce each party's sense of itself as one side of a contest, and obscure the influence of other states, international bodies and companies that do not fit the frame. The account above should be read with that distortion in mind.
